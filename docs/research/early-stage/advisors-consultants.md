# Advisors, consultants, mentors and investors as advice sources (2023-2026)

Purpose: inputs for the simulator's advice-sourcing system (advisors, consultants, mentors, accelerators, investors, peer founders). Figures are sourced where possible. Items marked [unverified] could not be confirmed in the sources I reached. Items marked [design] are game assumptions, not statistics.

## 1. Startup advisors

**Equity norms.** The Founder Institute FAST Agreement is the common template. Secondary sources describe it as indexing grants by stage and engagement, with a typical band of about 0.1% to 1% and a two-year monthly vest. Stage bands vary by source. One guide lists roughly 0.25% to 1.0% pre-funding, 0.1% to 0.5% post-seed and 0.05% to 0.25% post-Series A for a standard advisor, with strategic advisors higher ([Mintlify guide](https://mintlify.com/Ro5s/Startup-Starter-Pack/business/advisor-agreements); [LaunchRock](https://www.launchrock.com/library/expert-advice/advisor-shares)). Cliffs differ across sources (none, three months, six months) [unverified]. Check the current template at fi.co before using any band as fact.

**Observed grants (Carta).** Carta's public posts are the best available data, though they are short LinkedIn-style summaries with no visible sample sizes:
- Pre-seed median advisor grant about 0.24% for 2025, with 64% of advisors receiving under 0.3% fully diluted, and only about 1 in 10 at 1% or more ([Carta, Jan 2026](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-pre-seed-advisor-equity-0-24-percent/); [Carta 1% pushback post](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-advisor-1-percent-push-back-data/)).
- Seed median about 0.13% in Carta's May 2023 data ([Carta newsletter](https://carta.com/data/newsletter-preseed-advisor-equity/)). A figure of 0.12% circulates in secondary sources but I could not confirm it in Carta's own pages [unverified].
- Carta reports the trend as declining over several years ([Carta](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-advisor-equity-declining-over-time/)).

**Cash vs equity.** Most early advisors take equity only. Cash is usually reserved for paid, scoped work (a retainer for a specific project). Equity grants that are large relative to the stage are a warning sign.

**Time commitment.** Typical engaged advisors spend a few hours per month [design]. Sources rarely give a reliable figure, so treat "2 hours a month, one call" as the game default and "monthly board-style sessions" as the heavier tier.

**How founders find them.** Warm intros from investors, batch-mates, former colleagues and founders who already have a relationship with the advisor. Cold outreach works poorly for advisors [design].

**Share of advisors who are useful.** I found no reliable public statistic. Treat "most advisor relationships deliver little beyond introductions" as [design] until a source is found.

**Advisor shopping (collecting logos).** Founders who stack many advisors for signalling rarely get strategic time from them. Advisors have no fiduciary duty, unlike board members, and an advisory board can substitute for real governance [design]. Observer seats and advisor-with-side-letter arrangements compound later, because later investors cite them as precedent ([Allied VC](https://www.allied.vc/guides/startup-board-seat-allocation-governance-guide)).

**Conflicts of interest.** Advisors may hold stakes in competitors, sell services to the startup, or steer deals to their portfolio. Require disclosure in the advisor agreement [design].

**Pattern-matching and survivorship.** Advisors who built companies in a different decade, segment or cost structure pattern-match on what worked for them. Advisors who were lucky are remembered; those who failed are rarely asked. This is a well-known bias in founder advice, not a single cited study.

## 2. Consultants and fractional experts

**Rates (2025-2026).** Fractional CMO hourly rates are commonly quoted at $200-$350 per hour, with wider ranges of $150-$500 in 2026 guides ([MarketerHire 2025](https://marketerhire.com/blog/fractional-cmo-salary); [Go Fractional 2026](https://gofractional.com/blog/fractional-cmo-salary)). Monthly retainers cluster around $5,000-$15,000 ([Webtonic 2026](https://www.webtonic.io/blog/fractional-cmo-what-it-costs-in-2026)). The only independent survey I found is the FRAK State of Fractional (about 250 respondents), which reports 40% charging $5,000-$8,000 a month, 29.5% under $5,000, 18.5% at $8,000-$10,000 and 12% above $10,000 ([cited via Webtonic](https://www.webtonic.io/blog/fractional-cmo-cost-pricing-statistics)). Rates for pricing, fundraising, product, finance, legal-adjacent and SEO specialists were not researched in this pass [unverified; to be filled by a follow-up brief].

**Engagement shapes.** Common forms: a fixed-scope project (audit, pricing review, pitch review), a monthly retainer with a stated number of hours, and a fractional role with a fixed number of days. Fixed-scope projects with a named deliverable are easier to evaluate than open retainers [design].

**Quality distribution.** Quality is widely variable and hard to observe before hiring. Generic playbooks ("do content, do outbound") are common; specific advice that references the founder's data is rare. [design] Assume a stellar consultant is roughly one in five at the rate tier, with a wide gap to the median.

**Vetting.** Ask for named past clients in the same segment, specific before-and-after results with dates, and a reference who did not refer them to you. Ask the consultant to critique your own data on the first call. A generic answer is a signal.

## 3. Mentors and accelerators

**Evidence on accelerators.**
- Hallen, Cohen and Bingham (Organization Science, 2020, "Do Accelerators Work? If So, How?") compare accepted startups with applicants who narrowly missed admission across four cohorts. Accelerated companies generally did better, and the benefit depends on program design, especially whether mentor and customer contact is concentrated early or spread out ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2719810)).
- Gonzalez-Uribe and Leatherbee (Review of Financial Studies, 2018) study Start-Up Chile with a regression discontinuity design. Schooling plus services improved venture outcomes; services alone did not. This is quasi-experimental, not a randomized trial, and I found no Techstars RCT in this search [unverified].
- Avnimelech, Dushnitsky, Ellsaesser and Fitza (Strategic Management Journal, 2024, "Are Accelerators Akin to Breweries or Wineries?") find that a meaningful share of performance variation sits in the cohort (vintage) rather than in the accelerator itself, based on about 1,350 startups ([LBS repository](https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/3828)). Exact variance shares were not visible in my sources [unverified].
- Effect sizes are modest and selection-sensitive. Do not present any single accelerator as causing large outcome gaps.

**Mentor networks.** YC's office hours are a structured, frequent feedback channel; partners describe them as sessions where founders bring the previous week's results back ([Paul Graham essay](https://paulgraham.com/users.html); [YC blog](https://blog.ycombinator.com/yc-open-office-hours)). No controlled study of office-hours advice quality was found [unverified]. Techstars mentor matching is a stated feature of its programs; I did not verify its quality data.

**Conflicting advice.** Mentors disagree, and a founder who collects five opinions often ends up stuck. The fix is to ask each mentor a specific question with data attached, then decide yourself [design].

## 4. Investors as advisors

**Incentives.** Investors want growth and a follow-on round. Their advice tends to push for speed, scale and the next raise. That is a different objective from a sustainable business at lower ARR [design].

**Useful patterns.** Introductions to customers and hires, precedent from other portfolio companies, and realistic benchmarks for fundraising timing.

**Harmful patterns.** Pushing a raise before PMF, pressure to hit a growth number that the market does not support, and advice that serves the fund's portfolio rather than this company.

**Board dynamics at seed.** Common seed boards have one founder seat, one lead-investor seat and one independent, with a board of five often arriving at Series A ([Allied VC](https://www.allied.vc/guides/startup-board-seat-allocation-governance-guide)). An observer seat is harder to remove than it looks. A stats claim that only 34% of startups add a genuine independent director by Series A is attributed to an ECGI/NBER study, but I could not verify it [unverified].

## 5. Peer founders and communities

**Value.** Peers face the same problems at the same stage. They are useful for tactics (which tool, which agency to avoid), for pressure-testing numbers, and for honest reports of a failed channel.

**Pitfalls.** Survivorship: the founders who share in public groups are usually the ones doing well. Different context: a peer with a different segment, price or team size is not a comparable benchmark. Masterminds that charge fees are often sold on the strength of their members' stories [design].

**Pricing for masterminds.** I did not find a sourced survey of mastermind fees in this pass [unverified].

## 6. What makes advice good

Synthesis of the sources above, with [design] marks where the evidence is thin:

1. **Specificity to your context.** The advisor asks for your numbers before giving an opinion.
2. **Access to your data.** Advice from someone who has seen your funnel, cohorts or customer calls beats advice from a deck.
3. **Track record in the same segment and stage.** A B2B SMB founder gets little from an enterprise sales veteran, and the reverse is also true.
4. **Incentives aligned.** Cash or a clear stake in your success, with disclosed conflicts.
5. **Falsifiable recommendations.** Good advice names what would prove it wrong.

**How strong founders use advice.** They synthesize rather than obey. They ask for specific feedback ("what would you do with this cohort data?") rather than "any thoughts?". They use advisors for introductions more than for strategy, since introductions are the thing advisors can provide most reliably [design]. They keep an advisor list short and track what each advisor has actually done.

## Simulator implications [design]

- Advice sources should differ in cost (cash, equity, hours), availability (response lag), quality (tiered by track record and data access) and bias (investor growth push, survivorship, pattern-matching).
- Stellar advisor use: specific questions with data, follow-through, and reporting back. Mediocre use: collecting logos, taking generic advice, and equity grants too large for the engagement.
- Mistakes cost realistically: an oversized grant, a wrong hire on advice, or a pushed raise compounds into dilution, time loss or a bad board.
